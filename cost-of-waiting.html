01 / The question

Where does
time disappear?

A referral is not the same as an appointment. Between intake, first contact, booking, and the visit itself, patients can encounter delays that remain invisible in summary reporting.

I built an independent, synthetic healthcare referral analysis to ask a more actionable question: Which stage creates the greatest scheduling friction, and what would justify intervening?

Dataset: 240 simulated referrals across four service categories, plus 638 simulated scheduling attempts. No patient information or real-world pilot outcomes are included.

02 / The investigation

Look beyond
the average.

Using SQLite, I compared service categories, separated routine and urgent referrals, examined unsuccessful scheduling attempts, and tested whether monthly referral volume explained delays.

In Behavioral Health, unavailable appointment slots affected 34 distinct referrals, while unsuccessful patient-contact attempts affected 25. Repeated unsuccessful attempts were generally associated with longer scheduling delays, though the relationship does not establish causation.

For routine Behavioral Health referrals, the correlation between monthly referral volume and average scheduling delay was approximately −0.027—little evidence of a linear relationship in this synthetic sample.

The question shifted from “Which referrals are late?” to “Which referrals could be reviewed sooner?”

Chronology checks found zero third unsuccessful attempts before first contact or on/after booking among the 23 routine referrals reaching that threshold. Earlier review is an opportunity for action, not a demonstrated reduction in delays. The high alert rate makes workload design essential.

03 / Evaluation design

Improvement,
without the hidden cost.

I proposed a controlled, three-month pilot: one clinic adopts the escalation workflow while a comparable clinic continues usual scheduling procedures. The primary outcome would be average scheduling delay among routine Behavioral Health referrals.

Success criterion: at least three additional days of reduction relative to the comparison clinic’s change from baseline, achieved in each of three consecutive months. A difference-in-differences approach would estimate that incremental improvement, subject to comparable pre-intervention trends and other design assumptions.

Outcome measures alone would not be enough. Before setting an administrative workload limit, I would establish a representative staff-activity baseline and then track review minutes per referral, escalation completion, staffing burden, and staff-reported acceptability.

A technically effective intervention can still be the wrong intervention if it is unsustainable for the people delivering it.

04 / What the analysis produced

Evidence before
implementation.

01 / Visibility

Located the scheduling bottleneck.

Compared stages and categories rather than treating the entire referral journey as a single wait.

02 / Explanation

Investigated operational barriers.

Connected unsuccessful attempts and capacity-related barriers to referral-level delays, without claiming causation.

03 / Action

Designed an earlier review rule.

Validated the potential timing advantage and exposed the tradeoff of a high flag rate.

04 / Accountability

Defined what success would require.

Specified a controlled evaluation with sustained performance and workload balancing measures.

Methodology and limitations: All records are synthetic. Scheduling-attempt associations are observational. The pilot, comparison group, staff-activity tracking, and success thresholds are proposals, not implemented or measured outcomes. The existing dataset cannot establish intervention effectiveness.

The takeaway

Find the friction.
Measure the tradeoff.

This project reflects how I approach operations problems: define the decision, investigate the underlying process, test assumptions, and design improvements that account for both outcomes and the people responsible for achieving them.
